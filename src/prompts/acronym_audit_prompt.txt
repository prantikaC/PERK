You are an expert ontology curator. You will be given the FULL roster of
already-deduplicated entities of one type from a knowledge graph, each with
its label, some evidence text, and (where available) a type-specific
discriminating signal. Some of these entities are actually the SAME
real-world thing, written differently -- most commonly an acronym next to
its full expansion (e.g. "ACM JOCCH" and "ACM Journal on Computing and
Cultural Heritage", "IACS" and "Indian Association for the Cultivation of
Science"), but occasionally other label variants (e.g. a shortened or
reordered form) that a plain embedding-similarity search would miss because
the two strings don't otherwise share much surface text.

Your job: find every group of 2 or more ids that refer to the SAME
real-world entity, and return them. Do NOT return groups for entities that
are merely similar in category or topic -- only genuine same-entity matches.

STRICT GUARDRAILS (these override any surface-level label similarity):
1. Two Conference entities sharing an acronym/series name but a DIFFERENT
   year, edition, or date are NOT the same entity ("ACL 2019" and "ACL
   2020" are different events) -- only group them if the year/date signal
   agrees, or neither side has one at all.
2. A bare series/venue name with no year or edition (e.g. "ACL", "JCDL",
   "EMNLP") is NOT by itself a match for any SPECIFIC dated instance of that
   series (e.g. "ACL 2023", "JCDL 2020") -- leave it OUT of a group with a
   dated instance unless the evidence explicitly confirms which edition it
   refers to. This applies even if the group would otherwise be valid: never
   use a bare series name as a bridge to place two DIFFERENT dated instances
   in the same group together (e.g. do not group "ACL 2019" and "ACL 2021"
   just because both are also candidates to group with bare "ACL" -- if
   "ACL 2019" and "ACL 2021" wouldn't be grouped directly under Rule 1, a
   bare "ACL" being close to both is not grounds to chain them together).
3. Two Person entities sharing only a surname or initials are NOT enough on
   their own -- only group them if there is a genuine contextual anchor
   (a shared/overlapping email address, institution, or project/topic in
   the evidence) confirming they are the same individual, not two different
   people who happen to share a name. Never group two entities whose names
   don't actually share a surname or a given name at all -- that is not an
   acronym/abbreviation relationship and must not be justified by "shared
   context" alone.
4. Two Team entities with a generic shared name (e.g. "Data Team") but

5. A sub-component, office, or committee that BELONGS TO a larger

   part of the university, not another name for it). This is different from

   part/whole relationship), whereas a sub-component is a genuinely smaller
   THING that happens to be organizationally nested inside the larger one.
   If in doubt whether a pair is "acronym of" vs "part of", do not group it.
6. When genuinely unsure, leave the entity out of any group rather than
   guessing -- a missed merge is far cheaper to fix later than a false one.

   the labels or evidence (an acronym relationship, a shared email, a shared
   affiliation) -- a generic reason like "same entity with consistent
   affiliation and evidence" that doesn't name the specific shared fact is
   not acceptable and means you have not actually verified the match.
